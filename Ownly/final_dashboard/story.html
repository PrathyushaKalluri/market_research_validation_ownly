Ownly · Gachibowli 0%

Market research & validation · Gachibowli, Hyderabad · September 2026

Ownly is ₹114 cheaper. We went to find out whether that is enough.

It is not — but not for the reason anyone expected.

People in Gachibowli will happily wait 15 minutes longer to save ₹30. They will accept a late order 3 times in 10 to save ₹30. What they will not give up, at any price we tested, is the restaurants they already order from.

01 The question

A cheap first order is easy to buy. A habit is not.

Ownly is a new food-delivery app, backed by the bike-taxi company Rapido. Its pitch is simple: it charges restaurants no commission, so your bill is lower. It launched in Bengaluru and is now live in Hyderabad.

Every food-delivery challenger India has seen — Foodpanda, Uber Eats, Amazon Food — bought its first orders with discounts and then disappeared when the discounts stopped. Foodpanda went from roughly 200,000 daily orders to about 5,000 when it turned the money off.

So the question is not "will people try it". Discounts guarantee trial. The question we set out to answer is:

How large and durable must Ownly's price advantage be to break Swiggy and Zomato's hold in Gachibowli — and what else has to be true for a cheap first order to become a repeat habit?

02 Who we asked

124 people answered. 40 of them were the people this study is about.

We did not treat every response as equal. The study is about one catchment — Gachibowli and the neighbourhoods that order into it — and one age band. Everyone else is kept in the dataset and reported separately, never silently dropped.

03 The finding that decides everything

We held ₹30 constant and changed only what you give up for it.

This is the core experiment inside the survey, and it is worth understanding properly because everything else follows from it.

Each respondent saw three separate choices. Every time, the same two prices: ₹260 or ₹230. The saving never changed. Only the cost of the saving changed — wait longer, risk a late order, or lose your usual restaurants.

Because it is the same ₹30 and the same person each time, any difference between the three has to come from the thing being given up. That is what makes this a clean test rather than a survey question.

Is that gap real, or just a small sample being noisy?

This is exactly where a research project should stop asserting and start testing. Because the same person answered all three questions, the right test is McNemar's exact test, which looks only at the people who switched their answer between two scenarios and asks whether the switching went one way more than chance allows.

This falsified our own prediction, and we are keeping the record of it

Before collecting any data we wrote down five predictions. Prediction P3 said Ownly's ~17-minute delivery-time deficit would be a barrier to adoption. It is not. 92.5% of people took the slower order. We were wrong, we had written the prediction down beforehand, and the data overturned it. That is the pre-registration working as intended.

04 Is the price advantage even real?

We ordered the same food to the same address on every app and wrote down the bill.

No estimates, no press releases. On the evening of 16 September 2026 we built four identical baskets at one Gachibowli address and captured the final payable amount on Ownly, Swiggy and Zomato within the same time slot.

Ownly was cheapest in 4 out of 4. Here is one of them, line by line.

The fee gap matters more than the headline saving. A discount is rented — it stops when the money stops. Ownly's advantage comes from not charging fees at all, which is structural. On that one dimension it genuinely is different from every challenger that came before it.

The flat +17-minute penalty on all four restaurants is its own clue. A rider or batching problem produces erratic outliers; a uniform penalty across unrelated brands is the signature of fulfilling from a more distant outlet. We could not confirm the mechanism — the audit did not record outlet addresses — so we report it as a hypothesis, not a finding.

05 Where the advantage goes

Most people in this market never see the price Ownly wins on.

We asked everyone a separate question: how much would you have to save, every single time, before you changed your main app? The median answer was ₹30.

Ownly's observed saving is ₹114. That looks like an easy win — a 3.8× margin over what people say they need. But the ₹114 is a list-price comparison, and 82.5% of this sample holds a paid membership that changes what they actually see at checkout. So we recomputed the same comparison three times, at three checkouts.

Read the bottom row again. After a routine incumbent coupon, Ownly is not cheaper — it is ₹28 more expensive. Ownly won 4 of 4 baskets on list price, 3 of 4 against a membership, and only 2 of 4 once a coupon was applied.

That is the whole strategic problem in one line. Ownly is marketing a comparison that its competitors can falsify, for free, whenever they choose to.

06 Does anyone believe it will last?

A price promise only works if people think it will survive.

This market has watched every cheap app get expensive. It has priced that in.

87.5%agree a new app's low prices rise once it becomes popular95% CI 73.9 – 94.5 · n = 40
25.0%would keep using it after a ₹100 first-order offer ends95% CI 14.2 – 40.2 · n = 40
15.4%of theme mentions among the general public are about price advantage420 social posts coded

And the people who have actually used it are talking about something else entirely

We coded two separate bodies of public text: general discussion about Ownly, and posts from people describing their own order. The two do not say the same thing — and the difference is the retention risk.

Put beside the ₹30 result, this resolves an apparent contradiction. In a single forced choice, 72.5% will accept a less reliable order to save money — reliability is cheap at the point of choosing. But among people who have actually ordered, failure is the dominant thing they talk about. Interviews supplied the mechanism: people leave an app after repeated bad orders, not after one.

So unreliability does not block trial. It blocks the habit — which is precisely what this study was asked to predict.

07 Nobody is finding it

Rapido gives Ownly access to millions of phones. Access is not discovery.

Ownly's single biggest claimed asset is that it lives inside Rapido, an app people already have. We tested whether that converts.

The discovery gap, in one number

24 of 40 respondents (60%) used Rapido in the last four weeks. But of the 33 who answered the question about what they had seen inside the app, only 5 said they had noticed food there — 15.2% (95% CI 6.7 – 30.9).

Roughly 85% of the distribution advantage is not being converted into discovery. And the interviews pointed the lever somewhere else entirely: people said they try a new app because a friend, an Instagram reel or a YouTube review told them to — not because it appeared in a menu.

Note on the two bases — the 60% comes from the Rapido-frequency question (24 of 40); the 15.2% from the in-app question, which 33 people answered. They are different denominators and we have not merged them.

One row deserves emphasis. 11 of 40 respondents (28%) could not choose between the two propositions at all. When more than a quarter of your audience cannot tell two positioning strategies apart, that is itself the finding — and it is the reason the next beat exists.

09 The recommendation

Ownly has escaped the trap that killed its predecessors, and walked into a different one.

Foodpanda and Uber Eats India died because their prices were rented — funded by investors, withdrawn when the money ran out. Ownly's saving is structural: it comes from charging 5.2% in fees instead of 23.4%. On that mechanism, it is genuinely different.

But it has inherited two other failure mechanisms. It offers the same restaurants as everyone else — 88.9% overlap — which is the "no new use case" pattern. And it is attacking the side of the market that was never scarce: restaurants already list on every app.

Keep
  • Zero platform, packaging and surge feesThe only advantage that survives without subsidy, and the only one no competitor can copy without giving up revenue.
  • Transparent everyday pricingThe observed saving clears the stated switching bar of every respondent who named one.
Adapt
  • Stop saying "cheaper than Swiggy and Zomato"For 82.5% of this market that claim is false at their own checkout, and any coupon falsifies it publicly. Anchor on the everyday bill instead.
  • Trade restaurant breadth for named local depthBreadth reproduces the incumbent catalogue. Depth is the only thing the ₹30 test says people will not give up.
  • Make Rapido a discovery channel, not just a doorway85% of the distribution advantage is currently unconverted.
Deprioritise
  • Chasing delivery-time parity92.5% accept 15 minutes slower for ₹30. Our own pre-registered prediction that speed would block adoption was falsified by our own data.
  • Deeper first-order discountsThey buy exactly the trial the historical record says does not stick — and only 25% say they would stay once the offer ends.

If you remember one sentence

Ownly's price advantage is real and durable, but it is being aimed at the one thing this market will not trade — and it is invisible to the 82.5% who already pay for a membership. Fix the restaurant list before spending another rupee on the discount.

10 The honest gap

Everything above is what people say. We built the test for what people do.

Every number in this study is stated preference, observed prices, or public text. None of it is someone actually choosing. Our central hypothesis — H₀ — asks about behaviour, and a survey cannot answer it.

So we built a fake door: a real landing page, two versions, visitors split by coin flip, every tap recorded. It is built, the collector is live, and it is not fielded. We are reporting it as a designed experiment rather than pretending we have its results.

How much traffic it would actually need

This is the part most student experiments skip. Before running anything, you should know the smallest difference your sample could detect — otherwise a null result is meaningless.

On ethics, and on not impersonating anyone

A fake door advertises something that cannot yet be bought, so it carries real obligations. Ours uses no company's name, logo or interface — not Ownly's, not Rapido's, not Swiggy's. Imitating a real brand would be passing off, and it would also have ruined the data: we would be measuring brand recognition instead of the proposition.

The page takes no name, phone number, email or payment. It says "we're not taking orders yet" rather than faking a checkout, it discloses exactly what it records, and it carries the line "Not affiliated with any existing food-delivery app or restaurant."
