A challenger has none.

### 3.4 The cost components a challenger must fund

Swiggy's own definition of Contribution Margin (FACT, Glossary, Q1FY27 letter, p.~20):

> "Food Delivery Adjusted Revenue, less (i) delivery and other charges, less (ii) [platform-funded discounts and other variable costs]"

And Adjusted Revenue is defined as commission/fees from restaurant partners, fees from delivery partners for use of the technology platform, **subscription revenue (net of discounts, credits and free-delivery discounts provided through the Swiggy One membership programme)**, plus **fee from users**.

This confirms the four revenue legs a challenger must replicate:
1. Restaurant commission
2. Platform/user fee (see §2 — INR 17.58 at Swiggy as of Mar 2026)
3. Subscription (Swiggy One)
4. Advertising / promoted listings

> **A "zero-commission" model deletes leg 1 outright and, because it has no scale, cannot monetise leg 4 (ads) either. It must fund the entire delivery cost from legs 2 and 3.**

## 4. The incumbent moat — in the incumbents' own words

The single most valuable piece of evidence in this dossier. Swiggy's Q1FY27 shareholder letter contains a shareholder question explicitly about new entrants. Verbatim (FACT — https://www.swiggy.com/corporate/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Q1-FY2027-Shareholder-letter.pdf, p.10–11):

> **Q: "Recent headlines suggest potential entry of new competition in Food delivery. How resilient is your competitive positioning given these developments?"**
>
> "We believe our competitive position in Food delivery is durable with **no near term risks** to the underlying growth trajectory. We have been agile to evaluate possible opportunities across pricing, formats and even separate apps to open up the TAM further while maintaining the economics of the core platform. **The depth of our restaurant partner base and our tech and operational stack is fully built and scaled and we are well equipped to meet each need-state at a fraction of the cost a new player would incur**, while retaining our right to win with the relevant cohorts."
>
> "**Any new entrant must find a value vector that established platforms do not already serve at scale.**"

And on Toing, its own budget-delivery counter-launch:

> "We have been able to launch Toing **without any significant incremental fixed costs (unlike new competition who will have to build the entire stack ground up)** by leveraging our existing tech and execution capabilities. The initial investment in Toing is largely on new user acquisition which is **discretionary in nature and can be quickly scaled or cut-back** depending on the progress."

### 4.1 Why this is the core structural argument

| Incumbent asset | Swiggy's disclosed position | What a challenger must build |
|---|---|---|
| Restaurant supply | 265,800 monthly transacting partners; 270,000+ listed; 720+ cities (FACT) | From zero, city by city |
| Rider density | 648,000 monthly transacting delivery partners (FACT) | From zero; density determines cost/order |
| User base | 19.2 mn food-delivery MTUs; 27.5 mn platform MTUs (FACT) | From zero, against subscription lock-in |
| Tech/ops stack | "fully built and scaled" (COMPANY CLAIM) | Ground-up capex |
| Counter-launch capability | Toing to 50 cities at "no significant incremental fixed cost"; Bolt live in 700+ cities; 99-Store; Eat Right (~15% of volumes) | N/A |
| Ability to cut spend | New-user acquisition spend is "discretionary... can be quickly scaled or cut-back" | Cannot — it *is* the business |

> **The asymmetry.** The incumbent can replicate a challenger's *proposition* (speed, cheap meals, curation) at near-zero marginal fixed cost, because the stack already exists. The challenger cannot replicate the incumbent's *stack* at any price short of billions. Swiggy launched Toing into 50 cities as a defensive counter-product; Rapido/Ownly-class entrants must build 720 cities of supply to reach parity. And Swiggy's counter-spend is discretionary — it can be turned off the moment the challenger runs out of money, while the challenger's spend cannot.

### 6.1 THE DECISIVE QUOTE — the incumbent's verdict on zero-commission challengers

Eternal's Q1FY27 letter contains a shareholder question naming **Toing (Swiggy) and Ownly (Rapido)** directly. Verbatim, **Deepinder Goyal** (FACT — Eternal Q1FY27 Shareholders' Letter, p.3):

> **Q3. What has been the impact so far of Toing and Ownly on your business?**
>
> "The impact has been **limited**. These platforms are offering the **same restaurants, similar or longer delivery times, and lower menu prices funded by lower commissions and delivery fee — making the revenue gap even more unsustainable**. **There's no new use case being unlocked here.** The customer traction is **purely price-driven, and price-driven traction without structural economics tends to resolve itself**."
>
> "We've responded where needed and we'll adapt if something changes. But **we're not spending much energy on this**."
>
> "What we are spending energy on is **Bistro** — which is our answer to the question these platforms are pretending to solve. **If you want to make food delivery work at INR 50-150 price points, you can't do it without supply chain innovation.** We're rethinking kitchen operations from first principles here — designing custom equipment, workflows, and automation purpose-built for high-volume, limited-menu formats. This isn't a restaurant. It's closer to a **food manufacturing system**."

**Why this is the central finding of the dossier.** The market leader's diagnosis of the challenger model is that it is *not differentiated on supply* (same restaurants), *worse on service* (similar or longer delivery times), and *cheaper only because it is foregoing revenue* (lower commissions and delivery fee). That is not a competitive advantage; it is a subsidy. And the leader states it is **not reallocating resources to respond**.

Note also the *second-order* claim: Eternal argues the only way to genuinely serve the INR 50–150 price point is to **change the cost of the food itself** (vertically integrated micro-kitchens — Bistro), not to shave the platform's take. That reframes the challenge: the binding constraint at low AOV is not commission, it is the cost of producing and delivering a cheap meal.

> **Caveat (essential).** This is a **COMPANY CLAIM by an incumbent about its own competitors** and is self-serving by construction. It is FACT-grade only as evidence of *what Eternal said in a filing*, not as an objective assessment. However: (a) it is made in a document with legal liability attached, (b) it is corroborated by Swiggy's independent and near-identical assessment in §4, and (c) Eternal's own "Others" segment (Bistro, Nugget) **doubled its losses to INR 94 Cr** in the same quarter — showing the company is itself burning money on the low-price problem, which lends credibility to the claim that it is hard.

### 6.2 The "systemic trap" — pricing-led vs infrastructure-led growth

**Verbatim, Albinder Singh Dhindsa (CEO), Eternal Q1FY27 letter, p.5** (FACT). *Context: this answer concerns quick commerce, but the mechanism generalises and Eternal presents it as a general law of the category.*

> "competitive intensity remains high but has become **more predictable**. Our focus on long-term growth vectors remains unique in the market — we're the only player simultaneously investing in assortment depth, geographic expansion, and supply chain infrastructure while **competitors generally remain focused primarily on pricing**. **Pricing-led growth requires sustained cash burn, and leads QC companies into a systemic trap they can't easily walk out of** — that is visible in multiple QC players struggling to balance both growth and profitability. This is different from traditional e-commerce, where **discounting can be weaned off gradually, without hurting the business (much)**."
>
> "**Infrastructure-led growth, on the other hand, builds operating leverage** — each new store, each new category, each new city adds capacity that serves more customers at lower marginal cost. That's why we can grow rapidly and improve profitability at the same time."

> **The trap, stated plainly by the incumbent:** in on-demand delivery, unlike e-commerce, discounts **cannot be tapered without losing the volume**. A challenger that buys share with price has no exit from the subsidy. This is the mechanism by which challengers fail, described by the firm that has watched it happen repeatedly.

> ### THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT REGULATORY FINDING FOR A CHALLENGER
>
> **The CCI refused to treat high commissions as a competition problem.** In its April 2022 order the Commission declined to send "exorbitant commissions" for investigation at all, holding that commissions did not demonstrably harm *competition* (as distinct from harming *restaurants*). What survived was narrow and procedural: price parity, exclusivity, and self-preferencing.
>
> **Implication:** there is **no regulatory tailwind** behind a zero-commission challenger. A new entrant cannot expect commissions to be capped, and cannot expect the incumbents to be forced to raise prices. The one thing the case might eventually deliver — an end to exclusivity — would, if anything, *help the incumbents' restaurants multi-home*, which they overwhelmingly already do (Zomato's own claim: ~1% exclusive). **Restaurant anger is real; regulatory relief is not coming, and the exclusivity the case targets barely exists.**
>
> Note the asymmetry this creates: the entire "restaurants hate the commission" thesis, which is the emotional and commercial core of every zero-commission pitch, was examined by India's competition regulator and found *not to be an antitrust harm*.

> **The restaurant lock-in mechanism, from primary data.** Roughly 99% of restaurants are **not** exclusive — they already list on both Swiggy and Zomato. Multi-homing is the norm on the *supply* side, and it is free. This means a challenger can sign restaurants easily (Ownly onboarded ~20,000 in Bengaluru) — **but signing restaurants is worthless, because the restaurants are already on every platform.** Supply is not the scarce input; *demand* is. The challenger acquires a restaurant list identical to the incumbent's, which is precisely Deepinder Goyal's point in §6.1 ("These platforms are offering the **same restaurants**").
>

Restaurants will happily accept a free listing and keep their Swiggy/Zomato presence unchanged — costing the challenger all its revenue while changing nothing about consumer behaviour. The restaurant does not switch; it adds.

### 10.4 The density mechanism

Neither company discloses orders-per-rider-per-hour or delivery cost per order — **UNKNOWN**. But the mechanism is stated indirectly and consistently:

- **Eternal (FACT, Q1FY27 letter):** "growth in this business comes from making the platform more useful to more people, which **drives frequency, which drives density, which drives efficiency**. The flywheel doesn't ask you to choose." — Deepinder Goyal
- **Eternal (FACT, Q1FY27 letter):** "**Infrastructure-led growth... builds operating leverage** — each new store, each new category, each new city adds capacity that serves more customers at **lower marginal cost**." — Albinder Singh Dhindsa
- **Eternal (FACT, Q4FY26 letter):** the third of its "three pillars of long-term growth" is explicitly "**demand densification**."
- **Swiggy (FACT, Q1FY27 letter):** lists "**Densification** and warehouse/store automation" as worth **INR 5 per order** of contribution margin in its break-even bridge.

> **Density is circular and therefore self-reinforcing.** Orders per unit area determine how many deliveries a rider completes per hour; that determines cost per delivery; that determines the price the platform can charge; that determines order volume; which determines density. **The incumbent enters this loop at the top and the challenger at the bottom.** A challenger in one city with ~20,000 restaurants faces structurally worse cost per delivery than an incumbent with 265,800 transacting restaurants and 648,000 riders — *for the same rider wage*. This is why "we pay riders the same and charge restaurants nothing" does not produce a viable P&L: the challenger's cost per delivered order is higher, not equal.

## 13. Synthesis — the eight structural reasons challengers fail

Each is tied to a sourced finding above. Grades in brackets.

1. **The prize is a ~5% margin, and it takes ~16 years to reach.** Eternal needed 16 years to reach adjusted-EBITDA break-even and 18 years to reach $10bn NOV; steady-state guidance is 5–6% of NOV (Eternal) and 5% of GOV (Swiggy). Current actuals: Eternal 5.6%, Swiggy 3.1%. **[FACT — §1, §5.3, §6]**

2. **Zero commission attacks the non-scarce side of the market.** ~99% of restaurants already multi-home (Zomato's own submission to the CCI). A challenger's restaurant list is identical to the incumbent's, so free listings buy no differentiation — they only delete the challenger's largest revenue line. **[FACT/COMPANY CLAIM — §9.3, §6.1]**

3. **The regulator has already refused to treat commissions as an antitrust harm.** The CCI declined to investigate "exorbitant commissions" in its April 2022 order. No relief is coming. **[FACT — §9.1]**

4. **Density is circular, and the challenger starts at the bottom of the loop.** Both incumbents name densification as a primary growth pillar; Swiggy quantifies it at INR 5/order. A sub-scale entrant has a *higher* cost per delivered order at the *same* rider wage. **[FACT — §10.4]**

5. **The incumbents can replicate any challenger proposition at near-zero marginal fixed cost — and have already done so.** Swiggy launched Toing into 50 cities "without any significant incremental fixed costs"; Eternal cut Gold free-delivery MOV to INR 99 and curated sub-INR-250 meals from Q2FY26. Their counter-spend is explicitly "discretionary... can be quickly scaled or cut-back." The challenger's cannot. **[FACT — §4, §5.5]**

6. **Discounts in on-demand delivery cannot be tapered.** Eternal states this is the structural difference from e-commerce, and calls it "a systemic trap they can't easily walk out of." Foodpanda India's 200,000 → 5,000 daily-order collapse is the empirical proof. **[FACT + MEDIA — §6.2, §12]**

7. **Incumbent cohorts appreciate; challenger cohorts are rented.** Eternal's disclosed retention: 46% → 49% by Q12, NOV retention 279% by Q12, held "despite aggressive pricing from competitors." A challenger pays more CAC for a customer who retains worse. **[FACT — §6.3]**

8. **Fixed regulatory and compliance costs are regressive with scale.** Gig-worker welfare levies, GST on delivery charges from Sep 2025, the Labour Codes from Nov 2025, and multi-state litigation with court deposits — all payable out of profit the challenger does not have. **[FACT — §5.7, §10.3]**

### The one-sentence version

> Indian food delivery is a duopoly in which the winning margin is ~5% of order value, took sixteen years and billions to reach, depends on a self-reinforcing density loop the incumbents already own, and confers on those incumbents the ability to copy any challenger's proposition for free while the challenger pays full price for all of it — which is why the leader can say of the current crop of entrants, in a public filing, *"we're not spending much energy on this."*
