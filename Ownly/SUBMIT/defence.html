Read once, the night before

The twelve questions you will be asked, and the honest answer to each.

Every one of these has a real answer from your own data. None of them requires you to defend something indefensible — because you already wrote the limitations down yourself.

  1. Volunteer the limitation before they find it. "n = 40, so I'm reporting confidence intervals throughout" lands far better than being caught.
  2. "I don't know" is a complete answer when followed by "and here's what would tell us." That is what your "test next" list is for.
  3. Never defend a number you cannot source. Open numbers.html instead.
  4. Your strongest card is the falsified prediction. If the conversation stalls, play it.
On the sample
01Isn't n = 40 far too small to conclude anything?
Say

It's small, which is why I report a 95% confidence interval on every single proportion rather than quoting a point estimate. And the central finding doesn't depend on sample size in the way a percentage would — it's a paired design, so I'm comparing each person against themselves.

On that comparison, 24 people accepted a delay but refused to lose their restaurants, and exactly one did the reverse. A 24-to-1 split is significant at p = 0.0000015 even at n = 40. What n = 40 does limit is the subgroup analysis — and I've marked all of those underpowered rather than reporting them as findings.

Why this worksYou concede the real limit, then show the design was chosen to survive it.

02Your sample is 80% students. Doesn't that bias everything?
Say

Yes, and I'd flag it as the single biggest threat to generalising. 32 of 40 are students and only 7 work full-time.

I did test whether it changes the answer. Non-students took the cheaper, slower option 100% of the time against students' 90.6% — same direction, and Fisher's exact gives p = 1.00, so no detectable difference. The switching threshold does differ: students ₹30, professionals ₹50. But with n = 7 professionals I report that as directional, not as a segment finding.

Why this worksYou don't just admit the skew, you show you checked whether it mattered.

Don'tDon't claim the sample represents Gachibowli. It's a convenience sample and you should say so.

03Why did you throw away 84 of your 124 responses?
Say

I didn't throw any away — all 124 are still in the dataset carrying exclusion flags. I filtered to the study population in three documented steps: Hyderabad branch (78), age screen 20–35 (52), inside the Gachibowli catchment (40).

The study is about one catchment. Including Bengaluru respondents would have given a bigger n of the wrong people, which is worse than a smaller n of the right ones.

On the method
04This is all stated preference. People say one thing and do another.
Say

That's correct, and it's the single biggest limitation of the study. Nothing here is observed behaviour — it's what people say, what the apps charged, and what people wrote publicly.

Two things mitigate it. First, the trade-off design holds money constant and varies only the sacrifice, so it measures a relative preference, which is more robust than an absolute intention. Second, the price audit is genuinely observed — I built the same basket on three apps and recorded what the screen said.

For the part that stated preference genuinely can't answer, I built a fake-door experiment. It's on slide 14. It's designed, built, the collector is verified working, and it needs about a week of traffic I didn't have.

Why this worksThis is the question you most want to be asked, because your answer is "I know, and here is the instrument that fixes it."

05Four price baskets? That's nothing.
Say

Agreed — it's directional, not a price index, and I label it that way everywhere. One address, one Wednesday dinner slot, four baskets.

What makes it still useful is that it's a matched design: identical items, same address, same twenty-minute window, screenshots retained. So it's a clean read on the structure of the bill even though it's a thin read on the market. The structural point holds regardless of n — only ₹10 of the ₹71 gap was food price; the rest was fees Ownly doesn't charge.

06Why McNemar's test rather than a chi-square or a t-test?
Say

Because the same 40 people answered all three trade-off questions. That's paired data, and a chi-square or two-proportion test assumes independent groups — using one here would ignore the pairing and give the wrong answer.

McNemar looks only at the discordant pairs, the people who switched their answer between two scenarios, and asks whether the switching went one way more than chance allows. I used the exact version rather than the chi-square approximation because the discordant counts are small.

Why this worksNaming why the alternative would be wrong shows you chose the test rather than found it.

07Did you decide these tests before or after you saw the data?
Say

Before. There's a pre-analysis plan specifying the tests, variables and decision rules, and it's frozen with a provenance block.

I'd be precise about what that proves, though. The core sections are git-committed on 14 September, before the survey went live on the 16th — that's independently verifiable. A later section adding five predictions was written on the 18th and wasn't committed until afterwards, so its pre-data status rests on my own project log rather than on proof. I've marked those predictions "pre-specified but not independently verifiable", and no conclusion rests on them alone.

Why this worksMost students would claim full pre-registration. Distinguishing what you can prove from what you merely assert is exactly the habit being assessed.

On the findings
08Five of your nine tests found nothing. Isn't that a failed study?
Say

No — it's a study that reports its null results instead of hiding them. Three tests reject the null decisively, five fail to reject, and one couldn't be run.

And "fail to reject" doesn't mean "no effect". At n = 40 I can only detect large effects, so it mostly means this sample can't see one. That's why those rows are labelled underpowered rather than no relationship — and it's exactly how I know which questions need a bigger sample next.

Don'tNever say a null result "proves there's no difference." Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — and being precise about this is probably worth marks on its own.

09You predicted delivery speed would matter and it didn't. Doesn't that undermine you?
Say

The opposite, and this is the result I'd most want to highlight. I wrote prediction P3 down before collecting data: that Ownly's roughly 17-minute delivery deficit would block adoption. The data falsified it — 92.5% will wait 15 minutes longer to save ₹30.

A study that only confirms what it expected hasn't really tested anything. Committing to a prediction in advance and then reporting that your own data killed it is the whole point of pre-registration.

Why this worksThis is your single strongest card. If the Q&A goes quiet, play it.

10You say half of Ownly's orders fail. Where does that come from?
Say

I don't say that, and it would be wrong to. The 49.5% is the share of coded social posts by people describing their own order that mention a failure — it's a measure of what the conversation is about, not a failure rate.

People post about bad orders far more readily than good ones, so that number overstates failure. What's informative is the comparison: 49.5% among first-hand users against 18.9% in general discussion. The gap is the signal; neither level is.

Why this worksCorrecting a misreading of your own data, calmly, demonstrates you understand what it can and cannot support.

11Where are CAC, CLV, retention and market share? Those are basic marketing metrics.
Say

They're in the report, listed as NOT ESTIMABLE, each with a stated reason. Ten of them.

Every one needs Ownly's internal transaction or cost data — customers at the start and end of a period, marketing spend, variable cost per order. There's no public source, and a 40-person convenience sample can't stand in for a market. I could have produced plausible-looking numbers, but they'd have been fiction.

I'd argue that's itself a finding: the metrics that determine whether this business survives are precisely the ones no external researcher can see.

Why this worksThe instinct is to feel caught out. Don't — refusing to fabricate is the stronger position, and saying so plainly flips the question.

12So what should Ownly actually do on Monday morning?
Say

Fix the restaurant list before spending another rupee on discounting.

Specifically: keep the zero-fee structure, because it's the one advantage that survives without subsidy. Stop leading with "cheaper than Swiggy and Zomato", because for the 82.5% who hold a membership that claim is false at their own checkout and any coupon falsifies it. Swap broad chain coverage for named local depth, because 88.9% of the catalogue is already available elsewhere and restaurants are the one thing people won't trade. And stop spending on delivery speed, because 92.5% will wait.

Why this worksEvery clause carries a number. That's the difference between an opinion and a recommendation.

The hardest one
★You never actually tested your main hypothesis, did you?
Say

No, not behaviourally — and I'd rather say that plainly than dress up a survey result as conversion.

H₀ asks whether a Bengaluru-style proposition converts differently from a Hyderabad-localised one. I tested the stated-preference analogue: among the 29 who expressed a preference, 62.1% chose the Bengaluru pitch, p = 0.265 — I can't distinguish that from chance. And 11 of 40 couldn't choose between the two at all, which is itself informative about how distinct the two strategies really are.

Answering it properly needs behaviour, so I built the instrument: a two-arm fake-door landing test, randomised, with event logging. It's built, the collector is verified, and it needs about a week of traffic. At 200 visitors it would detect a 20-point difference; at 500, twelve points. That's the next thing I'd run, and I know what it would and wouldn't be able to tell me.

Why this worksYou can't win this by pretending. You win it by showing you know exactly what's missing, why it's missing, and what it would take — which is a more advanced answer than a result would have been.
